\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Philosophy.} The straight arrow carries Descartes' movement from thought to
existence~\cite{descartes}: what is entailed holds in every world where the premises hold
(\cref{thm:sound}). The circular arrow carries Hume's and Popper's view of induction: a
generalization is supported, never entailed (\cref{thm:hume}), and can be overturned by a single
counterexample (\cref{prop:popper}). Levels $l$ in the foundation give a place for Kant's
hierarchical structuring of thought~\cite{kant}.

\paragraph{Quantum analogies.} The quantum model of \cref{sec:quantum} suggests three further
analogies, which we state as analogies rather than results.
\emph{Complementarity}: as quantum objects show wave and particle aspects, reasoning involves both
abstract thought ($\varphi$) and concrete existence ($\exists$).
\emph{Uncertainty}: incompatible observables cannot both be sharp, which is a physical limitation on
what any study can reveal, distinct from Hume's logical one (\cref{prop:qhume}(4)).
\emph{Entanglement}: entangled systems show correlations that no classical model reproduces,
suggesting that logical relations between parts of a system need not reduce to relations between
the parts; these correlations cannot be used to signal, so they do not conflict with causality.
These views connect to the idea that physical processes are computations~\cite{deutsch,lloyd}.

\paragraph{Education and cognition.} The top row of the diagram is suitable for a first course in
logic; the foundation invites discussion of polarity and iteration. The visual layout follows
the view that diagrams make inferential structure explicit~\cite{larkin,johnsonlaird}.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} Each limitation of the first draft is listed with what
this version does about it; the items marked \emph{proved} are formalized in Lean.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Quantitative support (proved).} Support (\cref{def:support}) is qualitative.
        \Cref{sec:confirmation} adds Bayesian confirmation $P(t\mid s)>P(t)$ over a finite set
        of worlds and proves that it implies support, that a theory is confirmed by any genuine
        test it predicts (\cref{thm:hd}), that iteration raises confirmation
        (\cref{prop:ascend}) and that refutation sends it to $0$ (\cref{prop:qpopper}).
        \Cref{rem:probability} is now proved as well. \emph{Open:} infinite or continuous
        sets of worlds, where the same definitions can be stated with Mathlib's measures and
        \texttt{ProbabilityTheory.cond}; and the size of the confirmation, which depends on the
        prior even though its sign (\cref{cor:bswan}) does not.
  \item \emph{Ordered levels (proved).} \Cref{sec:levels} orders the levels and proves that
        iteration moves upward (\cref{thm:upward}). \emph{Proposal} for the other indices:
        let a hypothesis $h$ index a partition of $\W$, the distinctions a study under $h$ can
        draw; then a theory is identifiable under $h$ only if it is a union of cells.
        \Cref{prop:qhume}(4) is the quantum instance, where the $z$ basis cannot resolve the phase.
  \item \emph{Defeasible and higher-order reasoning.} Multi-premise defeasible inference is now
        treated in \cref{sec:defeasible} (\emph{proved}). For higher-order logic, note that every
        theorem above is proved for an arbitrary type of worlds, including function spaces: in
        the swan examples a world is a predicate on birds, so $\All$ already quantifies over a
        predicate. A higher-order \emph{logic}, with its own syntax and entailment, is still open;
        the natural route is Henkin semantics~\cite{henkin}, with soundness proved against the
        possible-worlds reading of \cref{sec:setting}.
  \item \emph{Noise (proved in a model; engineering open).} The quantum model is limited in
        practice by decoherence, state-preparation and measurement errors, and
        scale~\cite{preskill,arute}. \Cref{prop:noise} quantifies the effect of depolarizing
        noise on both arrows. \emph{Proposal:} formalize the three-qubit repetition code and show
        that it restores certainty to order $\varepsilon^2$; error-corrected and topological
        implementations, and hybrid quantum--classical reasoning, remain experimental steps.
  \item \emph{Section~\ref{sec:quantum} (proved, except physics).} Unitary deduction
        (\cref{prop:unitary}), non-distributivity (\cref{prop:nondistrib}) and invariance of von
        Neumann entropy are now formalized. Landauer's principle is a physical law, not a
        theorem of the model, and is assumed. The inequality $H(T\mid S)\le H(T)$ is a standard
        consequence of the concavity of entropy; formalizing it for finite distributions is a
        routine next step.
  \item \emph{Learners (empirical; proposed design).} Whether the diagram helps learners cannot
        be proved, only tested. We propose a preregistered randomized trial: students in an
        introductory logic course are assigned to a lesson using the diagram or to the same
        lesson in prose, then tested on classifying arguments as deductive or inductive, on
        spotting fallacies (affirming the consequent, hasty generalization) and on predicting
        the effect of a counterexample, with a retention test four weeks later. To detect a
        medium effect ($d=0.5$) with power $0.8$ at $\alpha=0.05$ needs about $64$ students per
        arm.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We have given a diagram for deduction and induction, a possible-worlds semantics for its symbols,
and machine-checked theorems for what it depicts: deduction is certain and transitive, negative
polarity is modus tollens, iteration narrows the supported theories, one counterexample falsifies,
and no finite study entails a universal theory. Realized in quantum information theory, the same
structure appears as certain measurement and unitary evolution on one side and state tomography on
the other, with the classical framework as the commuting case and with a new, physical limit on
induction. This suggests that quantum mechanics offers a natural physical setting for the
interplay of deduction and induction.
